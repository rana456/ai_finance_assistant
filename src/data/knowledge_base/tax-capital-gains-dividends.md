# Capital Gains and Dividends

In a taxable account, your investments can generate taxable income in two main ways: capital gains when you sell, and dividends while you hold. (Inside tax-advantaged accounts like IRAs and 401(k)s, this activity generally isn't taxed year to year.) Exact rates and thresholds change annually — verify at IRS.gov.

## Capital gains: short-term vs. long-term

A capital gain is the profit when you sell an investment for more than you paid. How long you held it matters a lot:

- **Short-term** gains (on investments held roughly a year or less) are generally taxed at your ordinary income tax rate — the same as your paycheck.
- **Long-term** gains (on investments held longer) are generally taxed at lower, preferential rates.

This is why holding period matters: selling too soon can turn a lower long-term rate into a higher ordinary one. If you sell for less than you paid, that's a capital loss, which can offset gains (see tax-loss harvesting).

## Dividends: qualified vs. ordinary

A dividend is a share of company profits paid to shareholders. For taxes, dividends split into:

- **Qualified** dividends, which meet certain requirements and are generally taxed at the lower long-term capital-gains rates.
- **Ordinary (non-qualified)** dividends, taxed at your regular income rate.

## Why this matters for account choice

Because taxable accounts generate this yearly tax drag, many people prefer to hold their most heavily-taxed investments inside tax-advantaged accounts, and use taxable accounts for more tax-efficient holdings. This idea is called "asset location."

## Key takeaways

- Capital gains are taxed by holding period: short-term at ordinary rates, long-term at lower rates.
- Dividends split into qualified (lower rates) and ordinary (regular rates).
- Tax-advantaged accounts shelter this activity; specific rates change yearly — verify at IRS.gov.
