# 401(k) Plans and Employer Match

A 401(k) is a retirement account offered through an employer. You contribute automatically from your paycheck, and the money is invested for retirement. Annual contribution limits are set by the IRS and change each year — check IRS.gov for the current amount.

## Traditional and Roth 401(k)s

Many plans offer both flavors. A Traditional 401(k) uses pre-tax contributions (a tax break now, taxed on withdrawal), while a Roth 401(k) uses after-tax contributions (tax-free qualified withdrawals later). Some people split contributions between the two.

## The employer match

Many employers match a portion of what you contribute — for example, adding money up to a percentage of your salary. This match is effectively additional compensation for saving. Because it's extra money you only get if you contribute, financial educators often describe capturing the full match as one of the highest-priority moves in personal finance: not contributing enough to get the full match leaves guaranteed money on the table.

## Vesting

Employer-matched money sometimes comes with a *vesting* schedule — you may need to stay with the employer for a certain period before the matched funds are fully yours. Your own contributions are always yours immediately.

## Early withdrawals

Because 401(k)s are meant for retirement, withdrawing before the qualifying age generally triggers income tax plus an additional penalty, with limited exceptions. The specifics change and depend on your situation, so verify at IRS.gov.

## Key takeaways

- A 401(k) is an employer retirement account, available in Traditional (pre-tax) and Roth (after-tax) forms.
- An employer match is essentially free money for contributing — capturing the full match is a common top priority.
- Matched funds may vest over time; early withdrawals usually incur tax and a penalty. Verify current rules at IRS.gov.
