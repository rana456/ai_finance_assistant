# Tax-Loss Harvesting and Tax Diversification

Two related ideas help manage the tax side of investing over time. Both are general concepts — the specific rules and limits change yearly and depend on your situation, so verify at IRS.gov and consult a tax professional before acting.

## Tax-loss harvesting

When an investment in a taxable account is worth less than you paid, selling it "realizes" a capital loss. Those losses can offset capital gains elsewhere, and a limited amount of net loss can offset ordinary income in a year, with the rest carried forward to future years. Deliberately realizing losses to reduce your tax bill is called tax-loss harvesting.

The main rule to know is the **wash-sale rule**: if you buy the same or a "substantially identical" investment shortly before or after selling at a loss, the tax benefit is disallowed. This is why harvesting is done carefully, often by moving into a similar-but-not-identical investment. Loss thresholds and the exact rules change — check IRS.gov.

## Tax diversification

Tax diversification means spreading your savings across the three tax treatments — tax-deferred, Roth/tax-free, and taxable — rather than putting everything in one. Because no one knows their future tax rates or what tax law will look like decades from now, holding a mix gives you options in retirement: you can choose which account to draw from to manage your taxable income year by year.

## Asset location

A related idea is "asset location": placing your least tax-efficient investments (those that generate lots of taxable income) inside tax-advantaged accounts, and more tax-efficient ones in taxable accounts. It's about *which account* holds *which investment*, to reduce overall tax drag.

## Key takeaways

- Tax-loss harvesting realizes losses to offset gains and some income; watch the wash-sale rule.
- Tax diversification spreads savings across deferred, Roth, and taxable accounts for future flexibility.
- Asset location puts tax-inefficient holdings in sheltered accounts. All rules change yearly — verify at IRS.gov.
