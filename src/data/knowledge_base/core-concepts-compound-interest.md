# Compound Interest

Compound interest is what happens when your investment earns returns, and then those returns start earning returns of their own. It is often described as "earning money on your money — and then earning money on that too." Over long periods it becomes the single most powerful force in building wealth.

## Simple vs. compound growth

Imagine you invest $1,000 and earn 10% per year.

- With **simple** growth, you'd earn $100 every year — always 10% of the original $1,000.
- With **compounding**, year one earns $100 (now $1,100). Year two earns 10% of $1,100, which is $110 (now $1,210). Year three earns 10% of $1,210, and so on. Each year's gain is larger than the last, because you're earning on a bigger and bigger balance.

The gap between the two starts small and grows dramatically over time.

## Why time is the key ingredient

Compounding rewards patience more than anything else. The longer your money stays invested, the more compounding cycles it goes through, and the later cycles are the largest. This is why starting early matters so much: someone who begins investing at 25 can end up with more than someone who invests more money but starts at 35, simply because their money had ten extra years to compound.

## The Rule of 72

A quick mental shortcut: divide 72 by your annual return rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 8% per year, 72 ÷ 8 = about 9 years to double. At 6%, about 12 years. It's an approximation, but a handy one.

## What this means in practice

- Start as early as you can, even with small amounts.
- Leave investments to grow rather than cashing out early.
- Reinvest returns (like dividends) so they can compound too.

## Key takeaways

- Compound interest means earning returns on your past returns.
- Time is the most important factor — early and consistent beats large but late.
- The Rule of 72 quickly estimates how long money takes to double.
