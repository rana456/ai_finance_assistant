# Taxable Brokerage Accounts

A taxable brokerage account is a standard investment account with no special tax treatment. It's what you open when you've used up tax-advantaged options, or when you want flexibility that retirement accounts don't offer. Tax rates on investment income change yearly — verify current figures at IRS.gov.

## What "taxable" means

Unlike a 401(k) or IRA, a brokerage account gives no deduction and no tax-free growth. Instead you pay tax as you go: on dividends and interest each year, and on capital gains when you sell an investment for a profit. The trade-off for this ongoing tax is freedom.

## The flexibility advantage

Taxable accounts have no contribution limits — you can invest as much as you want. There are no early-withdrawal penalties and no required withdrawals later in life; the money is available any time for any purpose. That makes a brokerage account well-suited to goals before retirement, or as a supplement once tax-advantaged accounts are full.

## Where it fits in the usual priority order

A common educational framework for ordering savings is: contribute to a 401(k) at least enough to capture any employer match, then fund tax-advantaged accounts like IRAs and HSAs, and use a taxable brokerage account for anything beyond that or for goals you'll reach before retirement age. This is a general framework, not personalized advice.

## Tax efficiency

Because brokerage accounts are taxed yearly, being tax-aware helps: holding investments long enough for long-term capital-gains rates, favoring tax-efficient funds, and using capital losses to offset gains all reduce the tax drag.

## Key takeaways

- A taxable brokerage account has no special tax treatment: taxed on dividends, interest, and realized gains.
- In exchange it offers full flexibility — no contribution limits, penalties, or required withdrawals.
- Often used after tax-advantaged accounts are full, or for pre-retirement goals.
