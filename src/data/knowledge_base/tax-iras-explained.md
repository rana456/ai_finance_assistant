# IRAs Explained

An IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement) is a retirement account you open yourself, independent of any employer. It's a common complement to a workplace 401(k). Contribution limits and income rules are set annually by the IRS — verify current numbers at IRS.gov.

## Traditional and Roth IRAs

Like 401(k)s, IRAs come in two tax flavors. A Traditional IRA may offer a tax deduction on contributions today, with withdrawals taxed as ordinary income later. A Roth IRA takes after-tax money now and offers tax-free qualified withdrawals in retirement. Whether you can deduct a Traditional IRA contribution, or contribute directly to a Roth, can depend on your income and whether you have a workplace plan.

## IRA vs. 401(k)

The accounts share the same Traditional/Roth tax logic but differ in structure:

- A **401(k)** is offered by an employer, may include a match, and usually has higher contribution limits.
- An **IRA** is opened on your own through a brokerage, has no employer match, typically has a lower contribution limit, but often offers a wider range of investment choices.

Many people use both: contributing to a 401(k) at least enough to get the employer match, then adding an IRA for more flexibility.

## Withdrawal rules

IRAs are designed for retirement, so withdrawing early generally triggers income tax plus a penalty, with certain exceptions. Traditional and Roth accounts also differ in required withdrawals later in life. These rules change and depend on your situation — check IRS.gov.

## Key takeaways

- An IRA is a self-opened retirement account, available in Traditional (pre-tax) and Roth (after-tax) forms.
- Compared with a 401(k): no employer match, usually lower limits, but more investment choice.
- Deductibility, income limits, and withdrawal rules change yearly — verify at IRS.gov.
