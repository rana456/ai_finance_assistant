# Diversification

Diversification means spreading your money across many different investments instead of concentrating it in just one or a few. The familiar phrase is "don't put all your eggs in one basket." If you drop one basket, you don't lose every egg.

## Why it reduces risk

Any single investment can perform badly for reasons specific to it — a company loses a lawsuit, an industry falls out of favor, a region hits hard times. When you hold many investments, one bad outcome is cushioned by the others. This company- or sector-specific risk is called **unsystematic risk**, and diversification is the main tool for reducing it.

## What diversification cannot do

Diversification does **not** remove all risk. It cannot protect you from **market risk** (also called systematic risk) — the risk that the whole market falls at once, as happens in a broad downturn. When markets drop broadly, a diversified portfolio usually drops too, just typically less violently than a single concentrated bet.

## Ways to diversify

- **Across many companies.** Holding shares in hundreds of companies instead of a handful.
- **Across asset classes.** Mixing stocks, bonds, and cash, which tend to behave differently.
- **Across sectors and regions.** Not putting everything into one industry (like technology) or one country.

## The easy path: index funds

For most beginners, the simplest way to diversify is a broad **index fund** — a single fund that holds a tiny slice of hundreds or thousands of companies at once. One purchase gives you instant diversification, which is why index funds are a common starting point for new investors.

## Key takeaways

- Diversification spreads money across many investments to reduce company- and sector-specific risk.
- It reduces unsystematic risk but not overall market risk.
- Broad index funds are an easy way to get diversified in a single step.
