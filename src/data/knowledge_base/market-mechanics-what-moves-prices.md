# What Moves Stock Prices

A stock's price is set by supply and demand: at any moment, it's the price where buyers and sellers agree to trade. When more people want to buy than sell, the price rises; when more want to sell, it falls. The interesting question is *what makes people want to buy or sell* — and a few forces dominate day to day.

## Company earnings and news

The biggest company-specific driver is earnings — how much profit a company reports, usually every three months. Prices often move sharply after an earnings report, and not just on the raw numbers but on how they compare to *expectations*. A company can post record profits and still see its stock fall if investors expected even more. Other company news moves prices too: new products, leadership changes, lawsuits, or big contracts.

## Macroeconomic news

Broad economic forces move most stocks at once. Interest-rate decisions, inflation reports, employment data, and economic growth figures all shift how investors feel about the future. For example, when interest rates rise, borrowing gets more expensive and future profits are worth less today, which often pressures stock prices across the board.

## Market sentiment

Beyond the numbers, prices reflect emotion and expectation — collectively called sentiment. Fear can drive selling even when a company's fundamentals haven't changed, and optimism can lift prices on hope alone. Sentiment is why prices can swing more than the underlying facts seem to justify, and why short-term moves are notoriously hard to predict.

## Why short-term moves are hard to predict

Because prices already reflect everything investors currently know, what moves them next is *new* information — which by definition isn't knowable in advance. This is why no one can reliably forecast day-to-day prices, and why long-term investing tends to focus on fundamentals rather than short-term timing.

## Key takeaways

- Prices are set by supply and demand, moment to moment.
- Day-to-day, the biggest drivers are earnings (vs. expectations), macroeconomic news, and market sentiment.
- Short-term price movements are very hard to predict because they depend on new, unknowable information.
