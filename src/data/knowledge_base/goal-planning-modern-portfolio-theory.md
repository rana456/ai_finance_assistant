# Modern Portfolio Theory Basics

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) is a framework for thinking about how to combine investments so you get the most return for the amount of risk you're willing to take. You don't need its heavy math to use its core ideas — the intuition is what matters.

## The key insight: diversification is close to a free lunch

MPT's central idea is that the risk of a whole portfolio isn't just the average of its parts. Because different investments don't move in lockstep — when some zig, others zag — combining them can reduce the portfolio's overall swings without necessarily reducing its expected return. This is why MPT is often described as diversification being the closest thing to a "free lunch" in investing: you can lower risk without giving up as much return as you'd expect.

## Risk and return move together

The second idea is that higher expected returns come with higher risk. There's no mix that gives you high returns with no volatility. Stocks have historically returned more than bonds, but with much bigger ups and downs. Choosing an allocation is really about choosing how much short-term volatility you're willing to accept in exchange for higher expected long-term growth.

## The efficient frontier (the intuition)

MPT describes an "efficient frontier" — the set of portfolios that offer the best possible expected return for each level of risk. The practical takeaway isn't the curve itself, but the principle: for any level of risk you're comfortable with, a well-diversified mix generally beats a concentrated bet. Piling into a single stock or sector usually means taking on extra risk that isn't rewarded.

## What this means for planning

- Diversify across asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash) and within them.
- Accept that more expected return means more volatility; choose a mix that matches your comfort and time horizon.
- Don't chase concentrated bets hoping for higher returns without the matching risk.

## Key takeaways

- MPT's core idea: diversification can reduce risk without proportionally reducing expected return, because assets don't all move together.
- Higher expected return always comes with higher risk.
- For any risk level, a diversified mix generally beats a concentrated one.
